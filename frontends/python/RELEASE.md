RELEASE_TYPE: patch

This release moves date and time generation into the engine, which now bundles a pinned IANA time zone database, and adds `timezone_keys()`.

`timezones()` and the new `timezone_keys()` draw IANA names from the engine's database, restricted to the names this Python's `zoneinfo` can load, so a saved failure means the same zone on every machine. Half the draws are weighted toward zones with a rare feature (a fractional offset, negative daylight saving, a skipped day), and a name shrinks down a ladder from `UTC` through the zones a reader recognises rather than alphabetically. Link names such as `Asia/Calcutta` are included and legacy names such as `EST5EDT` excluded by default; `links` and `legacy` switch each.

`datetimes()` with a `timezones` generator now draws the zone and the wall-clock time in one engine draw when the zone is a `zoneinfo.ZoneInfo`: one draw in twenty on average lands within a second, an hour or a day of one of the zone's daylight-saving or offset transitions, or of a well-known instant such as the Unix epoch or the end of the signed 32-bit epoch. A repeated wall time is drawn with either value of `fold`, where `fold` used to be always 0, and a skipped wall time is reached deliberately; `allow_imaginary=False` now makes the engine draw a real wall time instead of rejecting the test case. `datetimes()` also accepts timezone-aware bounds, as Hypothesis does: both bounds are then moments in time, every datetime is aware and lies between them in a zone drawn from `timezones`, which defaults to `timezones()` and must not generate `None`. A bound may be omitted (`min_value` and `max_value` now default to `None`); one naive and one aware bound is an `InvalidArgument`.

`times()`, `datetimes()` and `timedeltas()` draw whole microseconds, Python's resolution, rather than nanoseconds truncated afterwards. `dates()` targets the leap days nearest the bounds, the year boundaries, the 2038 and 2106 overflow days and ISO week 53 in one draw of twenty on average, and `timedeltas()` round durations such as a second, an hour, a day or a week; a `timedelta` now shrinks toward zero as a whole, so the simplest negative delta is a microsecond below zero rather than a whole day.
